### **Chapter 18: Social Systems and Institutions**

This chapter applies the Consensus Protocol of V1 Ch11 — in particular the Class 0 / 1 / 2 entity taxonomy (§11.4, §11.6) and the multi-agent coupling matrix $\kappa_{ij}$ (§11.2) — to the domain of social facts. Under the Part VI tier policy the treatment is a **Tier 3 social model with Tier 4 GCT readings**, not a derivation: the Tier 3 label belongs only to ordinary coarse-grained social-dynamics models with their own observables, and every mapping from GCT constructs onto those models is Tier 4. GCT does not generate which institutions a society will produce or predict a specific revolution, election, or market cycle. Throughout the chapter, *agent* denotes an empirically observed person or functional participant in a social model; it does not assign that person a derived Identity Polaron, physical Selection Operator, owner, or Level-II verdict. Social dynamics require empirically specified communication, incentive, coercion, storage, and transition kernels; Solenoid topology supplies none of them.

**18.1 Institutions as Class-0 Patterns with Quasi-Agency**

**18.1.1 The Ontological Question**

Sociology has long faced an ontological question that physical theory has had little to offer on: in what sense do governments, markets, religions, and corporations *exist*? Searle's distinction between "brute facts" and "institutional facts" (constituted by collective intentionality) is the contemporary standard reply; established positions range from Durkheimian social realism (institutions as *sui generis* social objects) to methodological individualism (institutions reducible to the beliefs and actions of their members). The Russellian frame developed in V1 Ch10 and the multi-Polaron Consensus Protocol of V1 Ch11 motivate a conditional structural reading distinct from each of these. [Tier 4 — GCT social-ontology translation]

**18.1.2 The Structural Reading**

An institution — a government, a market, a religion, a corporation — is modeled here as a *high-rigidity Class 0 pattern* physically instantiated in people, artefacts, records, routines, legal dispositions, and communication channels, and maintained by the coordinated activity of its constituent agent network. This classification is structural, not a derived phenomenal verdict: no institution-scale Identity Polaron, Selection-Operator mechanism, spanning unity domain, owner, or phenomenal bridge has been demonstrated, and the Tier-3 Polaron/Level-II bridge remains open for all substrates. The DMC/O.21/O.23/O.34 stack is only one Tier 3 candidate implementation and cannot establish an institutional-scale null. The structural Class-0 label therefore leaves institutional Level II **open (Tier 3)** rather than proved absent: no validated substrate criterion exists yet (it requires a typed, normalized, measured coupling, the Identity-Polaron construction and an independent phenomenal bridge; O.21/O.23/O.34, O.18). The optional Consensus-Action translation represents the network's measured coordination by $\kappa_{ij}$ and its effective pattern by $\Phi_{\mathrm{inst}}$; $\mathcal J[\Phi]$ is then a coarse-grained phenomenological model, not an institution derived from the 6D lattice or the Solenoid. [Tier 4 — GCT Class-0 / $\mathcal J$ mapping onto institutions and its phenomenal interpretation; only an ordinary coarse-grained coordination model with its own observables carries a Tier 3 label]

Throughout this chapter, $\kappa_{ij}$ must therefore be read as a phenomenological social interaction kernel, fitted to data. If one instead adopts the address-based form $\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_0e^{-\lambda_cd_H(i,j)}$, the address filtration, the assignment of addresses to persons, the address-to-coupling law, the exponential form, and $\lambda_c$ are all additional data requiring calibration, and the address-based form is a Tier 4 bridge hypothesis; common-cylinder membership alone has no social causal force.

**18.1.3 What "Quasi-Agency" Means and Does Not Mean**

Institutions exhibit *quasi-agency* because people, rules, records, and enforcement channels jointly produce actions attributed to the collective (a corporation hires, a government taxes, a market clears). This does not imply an institution-level subject. Nor does GCT presently show that individual decisions are produced by physical Selection Operators or coordinated through a multi-Polaron network; in the social model $\kappa_{ij}$ is a Tier 3 social-interaction surrogate, and any reading of it as multi-Polaron coupling is Tier 4. The institution is the *substrate* on which agent-level decisions take coordinated form, not an additional decision-maker layered on top.

This distinguishes the proposed reading from two positions in the social-ontology literature. Against group-mind realism (in which a collective acquires literal mental properties), the present model attributes no aggregate Selection Operator, quale, or Level II subject to the institution absent a separately demonstrated qualified unity domain. Against eliminativist methodological individualism, it treats the institution as *real* because its physically stored rules, resources, role relations, and enforcement channels constrain outcomes even as members change. That persistence depends on ordinary carriers and transmission dynamics, which an explicit physical memory/carrier bridge (not yet constructed) would have to describe; it is not stability conferred by a location, cylinder, or cohomology class in $\Sigma_\infty$. [Tier 3 social model]

**18.1.4 Stability, Decay, and Replacement**

Within the coarse-grained Consensus-Action model, the lifetime of an institutional pattern is modeled through the rate at which physical carriers and the constituent agent network reproduce it. Three useful mechanisms of institutional decay can then be translated as follows; they do not follow from Solenoid topology:

1. *Membership drift.* As highly engaged contributors are replaced by less engaged ones, institutional reproduction may weaken. $\kappa_{ij}$ and $\Phi_{\rm inst}$ are coarse-grained model variables, not rendering pressure. [Tier 3]
2. *Maintenance-cost escalation.* Administrative, material, cognitive, and enforcement costs rise as the institution fits its environment less well; members experience the pattern as increasingly effortful to maintain, which is what is colloquially called institutional sclerosis. [Tier 3] Calling the integrated burden "phason friction" is an optional Tier 4 translation; a physical derivation and calibration would be required before it becomes a measured cost ledger.
3. *Competitive replacement.* A rival pattern may become dominant when enough participants, resources, and enforcement channels shift toward it. Describing this as a lower-$\mathcal J$ consensus minimum is a coarse-grained optimization analogy [Tier 4 — GCT $\mathcal J$ translation], not physical Agent rendering or an energy collapse.

The framework predicts no specific institutional dynamics. A usable model must specify physical and social state variables, the physical carriers of institutional memory, and transition kernels; address nesting supplies none of these.

**18.2 Norms and Conventions as Multi-Polaron $\kappa_{ij}$ Equilibria**

**18.2.1 The Persistence Puzzle**

Social norms — traffic conventions, currency acceptance, etiquette, language norms, marriage forms — can persist even when individual deviation would be locally profitable, and can shift in coordinated waves. Game-theoretic accounts treat norms as equilibria of repeated coordination games. GCT proposes an optional translation in which such an equilibrium is represented by a stationary point of the phenomenological Consensus Action Functional of V1 §11.3.1; it does not derive the equilibrium from topology. [Tier 4 — GCT translation]

**18.2.2 The Variational Statement**

Recall from V1 §11.3.2 that the stationarity condition for the consensus potential, in the high-redundancy regime where the entropic correction is suppressed by the total coupling weight, is

$$\Phi_{\text{local}} = \frac{\sum_j \kappa_{ij}\, \Phi_j}{\sum_j \kappa_{ij}}$$

Read as a coarse-grained coordination model, this is the familiar weighted-averaging consensus rule of opinion-dynamics models, and in that ordinary sense a social norm is represented by a region of the effective potential $\Phi$ in which a coordinated practice (drive on the right, accept fiat currency, use this phoneme system) is a local minimum of $\mathcal{J}[\Phi]$ given a calibrated $\kappa_{ij}$ matrix. [Tier 3 — ordinary social-dynamics model] The equation is a model of observed coordination costs. A norm's actual persistence depends on incentives, learning, records, sanctions, network structure, and other physical and social kernels; neither a common address cylinder nor $d_H$ makes it persist.

If a microscopic bridge from agent-level energetic work to the social variables were derived, the model could supply a physical cost ledger rather than merely relabel utility. That bridge is presently open. The comparison between the "weight" of social conformity and Selection-Operator drag against $\Phi_\perp$ (V1 §16.2.3) is therefore a Tier 4 phenomenological translation, not an identity or scale-extension theorem.

**18.2.3 When Norms Change**

Within the coarse-grained potential model, a norm shift is represented when the fitted maintenance cost of the existing equilibrium exceeds the modeled barrier to a competing equilibrium. Three heuristic regimes are useful:

1. *Gradual erosion.* The $\kappa_{ij}$ network supporting the norm decays slowly (members exit, cohorts age out, the pattern stops being transmitted). The norm fades without a discrete transition. [Tier 3]
2. *Coordinated cascade.* A tightly coordinated subnetwork can tip a norm through ordinary organization, communication, and threshold effects. [Tier 3] In the optional super-radiant analogy of V1 §11.8, such a phase-coherent subnetwork is represented by quadratic-in-$N$ rather than linear rendering pressure on a competing $\Phi$ configuration. This scaling is not established for social networks: it requires a defined amplitude, phase observable, and calibrated outcome kernel. [Tier 4 — phenomenological analogy]
3. *Exogenous shock.* An environmental change (technological substrate shift, demographic discontinuity, external coercion) raises the cost of maintaining the existing norm above what any internal-network adjustment can buffer. The norm fails by external loading rather than internal succession. [Tier 3]

In the model, relative support for competing $\Phi$ minima, weighted by the calibrated $\kappa_{ij}$ matrix, is one candidate summary variable. It determines an outcome only inside a validated transition model; the framework neither supplies that kernel nor predicts which regime a given norm-change will follow. [Tier 3]

**18.2.4 Norms versus Institutions**

The boundary between a norm (§18.2) and an institution (§18.1) is not sharp in the model. Both are treated as Class 0 patterns maintained by physical agent networks. The distinction is one of rigidity and codification: an institution typically carries explicit machinery (rules, charters, archives, resources, enforcement) distributed across multiple physical carriers, whereas a norm may rest mainly on tacit coordination. Treating both as Class 0 patterns and calling the former a higher-barrier minimum is a GCT model translation. [Tier 4 — Class-0 / barrier translation; the codification distinction itself is ordinary sociology]

**18.3 Revolutions, Phase Transitions, and Reconfiguration Dynamics**

**18.3.1 Revolutions as Consensus De-Rigidification**

In the coarse-grained model, a political revolution may be represented as a phase transition in the physical and social reproduction of an institutional pattern. Below the modeled threshold, the incumbent is a local minimum of $\mathcal{J}[\Phi]$; above it, a rival organization becomes the minimum and the prior pattern loses carriers and enforcement. This is a translation of empirically specified social dynamics, not a transition theorem derived from the Solenoid or the icosahedral lattice. [Tier 3 for an ordinary threshold/phase-transition social model; Tier 4 for its $\mathcal J[\Phi]$ reading]

**18.3.2 Threshold Conditions**

Conditional on the phenomenological potential model, a revolutionary threshold can be sketched as the point where the measured cost of maintaining the incumbent network exceeds the modeled barrier to a competing organization. The physical and social transition kernel must still be supplied. Three candidate factors are:

1. *Internal coherence of the challenger configuration.* A tightly coordinated movement may lower the fitted barrier through ordinary organization and communication. Representing this by $N^2$ super-radiant rendering pressure (V1 §11.8) is a Tier 4 phenomenological analogy that would require a derived amplitude and phase-locking observable; it is not a social scaling theorem.
2. *Decoupling of prevailing enforcement.* If the high-$\kappa_{ij}$ enforcement subnetwork (security forces, key administrators) reduces its coupling to the institutional pattern, the fitted institutional-support variable can fall faster than formal membership alone would suggest.
3. *Exogenous loading.* Famine, war, contagion, technological disruption, or external coercion increase the cost of maintaining the prior pattern.

If a calibrated model shows that the combined loading exceeds its fitted barrier, the consensus may tip toward a new pattern. The framework does not predict *when* this will happen for any specific institution — that is the empirical work of political science and historical sociology — and the listed quantities are candidate observables, not topology-derived sufficient conditions. [Tier 3]

**18.3.3 Post-Revolutionary Reconfiguration**

The aftermath of a phase transition is not the immediate stability of the successor organization. In the coarse-grained potential model, several candidate minima can compete while people, records, resources, and enforcement channels are redistributed. The actual relaxation is governed by the participating agents' physical states and by social transition kernels, not by a trajectory through $\Sigma_\infty$. Historical cases can be described with the multi-basin analogy, but the framework does not generate their mechanisms or outcomes. [Tier 3]

**18.4 Distinction from Individualist Consciousness Theories**

Other consciousness theories — Integrated Information Theory (V1 §17.5), Higher-Order Theories (V1 §17.6.3), Global Workspace Theory (V1 §17.6.2), Orchestrated Objective Reduction (V1 §17.6.1) — operate at the level of the individual subject and offer no native machinery for institutional, normative, or revolutionary dynamics. Most are silent on social ontology because their formalism does not extend to it; IIT is the exception, since its exclusion postulate explicitly denies consciousness to aggregates that are not maxima of integrated information.

GCT's multi-Polaron Consensus Protocol supplies a common notation for agents, interaction weights, and coarse-grained patterns. Applying it to institutions is nevertheless an analogy-bearing Tier 4 bridge: a social institution is not thereby the same mathematical object as a microscopic lattice defect (V1 §11.4.1); the common-commutant description of compatible records (V1 §11.13.3; Tier 1 algebra once the updates are supplied, Tier 3 application) does not derive a social equilibrium; and the Polaron algebraic-unity candidate (V1 §11.12) does not derive a revolution. Any claimed scale extension must provide the physical state variables, coarse-graining map, physical carriers of institutional memory, and transition kernels.

The framework's present grip on social ontology is therefore a substrate-compatible vocabulary, not a derivation of institutional existence, persistence, value, or history. [Tier 4 for the GCT reading]

**18.4.1 What This Chapter Does Not Claim**

The chapter offers a structural reframing of social facts within GCT. It does not claim the following:

- It does not derive specific institutions from the icosahedral geometry. Which institutions emerge in a given society is set by historical, ecological, and biographical inputs that the framework does not generate.
- It does not assign Level II apperception to institutions. The structural reading of §18.1 models institutions as Class 0 patterns whose rigidity depends on constituent agents; no institution-scale Selection Operator, owner, phenomenal bridge, or normalized rate pair has been demonstrated. Institutional Level II is therefore **open (Tier 3)** and no $\eta_{Zeno}$ value is assigned; neither is established as zero.
- It does not predict the trajectory of any specific revolution, election, or market cycle. The threshold-condition sketch of §18.3.2 identifies which quantities, in principle, would have to be measured, not what their values are in any historical case.
- It does not derive institutional durability from Solenoid topology. Longevity requires physically persistent records, practices, resources, succession, and transmission channels, described by a physical carrier bridge and a historical transition model.
- It does not derive legitimacy, moral authority, goodness, sinfulness, or any other evaluation from address proximity, nested cylinders, coupling strength, low modeled friction, or longevity. Descriptive persistence and normative warrant are different questions.
- It does not adjudicate among political philosophies. The framework is compatible with liberal, conservative, socialist, and many other first-order political positions; its vocabulary constrains the modeled ontology of institutions, not their evaluation.
